A practical guide for product teams

EU EMC compliance starts with good design choices.

A clear guide to harmonised standards, CE marking, and the decisions that help an electrical or electronic product reach the European market with fewer surprises.

What matters mostChoose the right legal route early. Define where and how the product will be used. Test a realistic configuration and keep clear records of the decisions behind it.

A product can work perfectly on the bench and still interfere with nearby equipment. It can also be affected by the electromagnetic environment around it. Europe’s EMC rules address both situations.

The real challenge is rarely finding a standard. It is linking the product’s functions, ports, cables, power supply, installation conditions, and intended environment to a test plan that makes sense. When this is done early, the team has more time to solve issues and the route to CE marking is much clearer.

Important distinctionHarmonised standards are a recognised way to show conformity. They do not remove the manufacturer’s responsibility or replace a complete conformity assessment.

Start with the legal framework, then plan the testing

The EMC Directive (2014/30/EU) is usually the starting point for electrical and electronic products without radio functions. It is not always the only legislation involved. Wireless products commonly fall under the Radio Equipment Directive. Equipment connected to mains power can raise Low Voltage Directive and power quality questions. Medical products, automotive equipment, machinery, and other sectors may follow more specific routes.

Understanding standards

Where harmonised standards fit

Harmonised European standards are developed by recognised bodies such as CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI. When their references are published in the Official Journal of the European Union and they are correctly applied, they can support presumption of conformity for the requirements they cover.

Generic EMC standards

Generic parts of the EN 61000 series are often used when no specific standard for the product applies. The choice between residential, light industrial, and industrial environments matters because the expected disturbances and permitted emissions are different.

Power quality standards

For equipment connected to public AC mains, relevant parts of the EN 61000 series address harmonic current, voltage fluctuation, and flicker. The right part depends on the product and its current characteristics.

Standards for product types

Where one applies directly, a product standard usually offers a better fit than a generic standard. Examples cover multimedia equipment, measurement and control equipment, lighting, household appliances, and industrial products.

Radio and specialised products

Radio equipment usually needs both EMC and radio spectrum evaluation under RED standards. Medical and automotive equipment may follow standards for their particular sector and performance needs.

Bring EMC into the development process

The best time to start is while the design can still change. Looking early at grounding, cable design, shielding, power conversion, enclosure seams, operating modes, and monitoring criteria can prevent expensive changes later. Helpful early checks include radiated emissions testing, conducted emissions testing, and an initial pre compliance review.

Phase 01

Plan

Understand the requirements and choose standards that fit the product.

Phase 02

Investigate

Use EMC pre compliance testing to find problems while the design is still flexible.

Phase 03

Validate

Carry out formal EMC testing with a controlled setup and clear performance criteria.

Phase 04

Maintain

Review component, firmware, manufacturing, and installation changes after launch.

A practical EU EMC project checklist

  • Identify all applicable directives and standards for the product.
  • Confirm the intended electromagnetic environment and installation conditions.
  • Document the operating modes most likely to create the highest emissions and the accessories that represent normal use.
  • Consider emissions, radiated immunity, ESD, EFT, surge, dips, and power quality needs as applicable.
  • Run early checks while there is still time to make useful design changes.
  • Define what acceptable performance looks like during immunity testing.
  • Keep reports, drawings, risk assessment, instructions, labels, and design evidence together.
  • Issue an EU Declaration of Conformity only after the full assessment is complete.
  • Review standard updates and material changes throughout the life of the product.
  • Keep the technical documentation ready in case market authorities request it.
